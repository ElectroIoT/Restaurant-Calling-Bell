SI24R1 Calling Bell

Press a coin-cell button and the receiver plays that button's tune, shows its number and sends back a "call received" acknowledgement. Built for restaurant tables, home or elderly calls, and shop doorbells.

How it works

Many button nodes talk to one receiver. Every press is one short radio packet. The receiver replies inside the radio's ACK, so the button can show the call was heard.

Button node

ATtiny85 + SI24R1 + CR2032

Sleeps at under 2 µA. Wakes on press, sends ID, waits for ACK, blinks LED, sleeps.

call packet→←ACK status

2.4 GHz link

Channel 76, 250 kbps

Auto-ACK with payload, 5-byte address, retries with random delay.

UART (later)→ESP32 / phone

Receiver

STM32C031F6P6 + SI24R1

Queues calls, shows number, plays tune or voice, drives a relay output.

Button side

transmitter

One small board per table or room. Priority is battery life and a tiny size.

Parts

  • ATtiny85-20SU (SOIC-8) or DIP-8 for testing
  • SI24R1 module (nRF24L01+ footprint)
  • CR2032 holder, or 2×AAA for longer life
  • 1 tactile button, 1 LED + 1 kΩ
  • 10 µF + 100 nF across the radio supply
  • Optional: 3.3 V LDO if using 2×AAA

Why ATtiny85

  • USI port runs the SPI the radio needs
  • Power-down sleep with pin-change wake
  • 8 KB flash is plenty for this firmware
  • ATtiny13 was ruled out: 1 KB flash, 64 B RAM, too few pins

Behaviour

  • Press wakes the chip, 20 ms debounce
  • Short press = normal call, long press (1 s) = "bill please", double press = urgent
  • Sends up to 5 tries with a random 5–40 ms gap
  • ACK received: LED blinks 2×. No ACK: LED stays solid for 1 s
  • 3 s lockout against repeat presses

A CR2032 (about 220 mAh) at 50 presses a day lasts around 2 years, limited mostly by the battery's own self-discharge. Estimate only; measure on the real board.

Battery options (fit one, never both)

Both cells are about 3 V, so the radio and ATtiny85 run straight from them with no regulator. The board carries pads for a CR2032 holder and a 2×AAA holder; solder only one.

OptionLife (50 presses/day)Use when
CR2032about 2–3 yearsSmallest node, stuck under a table or on a stand
2×AAA8+ yearsEasy swaps by staff, longer life, steadier voltage
  • Voltage window: 3.2 V fresh alkaline to about 2.0 V empty; the radio needs at least 1.9 V and the brown-out is set to 1.8 V. Fresh lithium AAAs sit near 3.6 V, the radio's limit, so prefer alkaline.
  • Reverse-polarity protection: a P-channel MOSFET, not a diode, so a 0.3 V diode drop does not waste cell range.
  • The node measures its own supply against the internal 1.1 V reference and sends it as the battery byte. The low-battery flag sets at 2.3 V.
  • Sleep current decides battery life, so the firmware turns brown-out detection off during sleep and powers the radio down after every press.
Brown-out matters on a coin cell. Fuse BOD to 1.8 V and keep the 10 µF capacitor right at the radio, or the radio resets mid-packet when the cell is cold or old.

Receiver side

central unit

One unit near the kitchen, counter or reception. It listens all the time, so it runs on 5 V mains.

Parts

  • STM32C031F6P6 (TSSOP20) on a mini dev board
  • SI24R1 module + 10 µF at its supply
  • PAM8403 amplifier board + 8 Ω 3 W speaker
  • TM1637 4-digit display for the table number
  • "Served" push button, status LED
  • 5 V 1 A adapter + 3.3 V LDO for the radio
  • Optional: DFPlayer Mini, relay, ESP32

Why STM32C031

  • 48 MHz Cortex-M0+, 32 KB flash, 12 KB RAM
  • Hardware SPI, UARTs and timers cover radio, voice and PWM audio with no sharing
  • Enough room for a 32-entry call queue and a melody table
  • STM8S003F3 also works here if you want the smaller chip: drop the queue to 8 entries and skip voice

Behaviour

  • Always in RX with auto-ACK
  • New call: ACK status "queued", play that node's tune, show number
  • Same node again within 3 s: ignored
  • Repeat chime every 30 s until served
  • "Served" button clears the oldest call; next call shows
  • Low-battery flag from a node lights a warning LED

Sound and amplifier

PAM8403
STM32 PWM pin ─ 1 kΩ ─┬─ 1 µF ─ PAM8403 L-in │ 10 nF │ GND PAM8403 L+ ──► speaker (+) PAM8403 L− ──► speaker (−) Never connect a speaker terminal to GND (bridge-tied output). 5 V rail: 100 µF + 100 nF at the amp. Unused channel input: 1 µF to GND.
  • Tones and melodies: a note table plays through PWM. Each node ID gets its own tune; urgent calls use a fast beep pattern.
  • Short samples: the C031 has no DAC, so PWM plus DMA plays about 2–3 s of 8-bit audio from flash.
  • Voice ("Table five calling"): a DFPlayer Mini on USART1 plays numbered files from a microSD card. Wire its DAC output to the PAM8403 for more volume.
  • No hiss between calls: pull the PAM8403 mute pin low through PB6 when idle.
  • Speaker: 8 Ω, 2–3 W, 40–57 mm, in a small enclosure.

Build order

Each step proves one thing before the next is added.

  1. Radio link. Arduino or Nano sketch on both ends. Send a counter, read it back through the ACK payload. This confirms both SI24R1 modules and your power filtering.
  2. Receiver on STM32C031. SPI1 driver, receive a packet, print the node ID on a serial port.
  3. Button on ATtiny85. USI SPI driver, one press sends one packet, then sleep. Measure sleep current with a multimeter.
  4. ACK round trip. Receiver replies "received"; the button LED blinks.
  5. Audio. PWM tone through the PAM8403, then per-node melodies, then the mute pin.
  6. Display, queue and Served button. TM1637 shows the table number, repeat chime every 30 s.
  7. Range and battery test. Walk through walls, then log a week of presses.
  8. Extras. DFPlayer voice, relay output, ESP32 alerts to phone or to RestoProERP.

Risks to check early

Power

Radio resets on a weak coin cell. Fix with the 10 µF capacitor and 1.8 V brown-out.

Noise

PAM8403 switching noise can hurt reception. Give the radio its own 3.3 V regulator and keep it away from the speaker wires.

Clones

Some SI24R1 modules differ slightly from the nRF24L01+ on register defaults. Write every register explicitly instead of relying on reset values.

Collision

Two simultaneous presses are handled by the random retry delay. Test with 5 buttons pressed together.

Plan v1. Pin choices and current figures are estimates until tested on hardware.
